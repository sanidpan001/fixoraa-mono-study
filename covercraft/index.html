Do cover letters still matter?

Yes — but not the way they used to. Fewer hiring managers read every letter word by word, yet the cover letter remains the only part of your application written in your voice. Recruiters consistently report using it as a tiebreaker: when two CVs look equal, the candidate who wrote a specific, thoughtful letter wins. A generic letter, on the other hand, is worse than none — it signals low effort.

The good news: a strong cover letter is a formula, not a talent. Specific role, specific company, three proof points, confident close. CoverCraft builds exactly that structure from your inputs, so you spend your energy on the content that matters instead of the blank page.

The anatomy of a letter that gets read

1. The opening: role + why this company

Two sentences. Name the exact role title and one genuine reason you're drawn to this company — a product you admire, a value you share, a problem you want to solve. "I'm excited to apply for the Senior Designer role at Acme" beats "I am writing to express my interest in the advertised position" every time. Specificity is the whole game.

2. The body: three proof points

Pick your three strongest, most relevant achievements — not your life story. Each point should follow the pattern: what you did, the scale, the result. "Led a rebrand across 40 markets, lifting brand recall 18%" is a proof point. "I'm a creative team player" is not. Numbers beat adjectives; specifics beat claims.

3. The close: confidence + next step

End by connecting your points to their needs and stating you're keen to discuss the role. "I'd welcome the chance to discuss how my experience with X can help Acme achieve Y" is confident without grovelling. Never apologise for your experience level, never beg, and never write "I know I don't have X but…" — frame what you bring.

Choosing your tone

Professional is the default: formal, polished, safe for finance, law, healthcare and large corporates. Friendly works for startups, creative roles, nonprofits and customer-facing jobs — warm and human while staying competent. Confident suits senior roles and sales: direct, achievement-led, unapologetic about ambition. When in doubt, match the tone of the company's own website and job ad — mirror their language back to them.

Length: the 250–350 word rule

Hiring managers spend under a minute on first pass. Aim for 250–350 words: roughly four short paragraphs. Every sentence must earn its place — if a sentence could appear in anyone's letter, delete it. CoverCraft's length check keeps you in the zone: too short looks careless, too long won't get read.

Mistakes that kill applications

Repeating your CV: the letter should add narrative and motivation, not duplicate bullet points. Wrong company name: the fastest rejection in hiring — proofread the header twice. Apologising: "Although I lack direct experience…" tells them what to reject you for. Novel-length letters: over 400 words signals you can't prioritise. Flattery without substance: "I've always admired Acme" means nothing without a specific reason.

After you generate: the 5-minute polish

Read the letter aloud — awkward phrasing reveals itself instantly. Check every claim against your CV for consistency. Cut one sentence (there's always one). Verify the hiring manager's name and title if you use one. Save as PDF with a clean filename: Firstname-Lastname-Cover-Letter.pdf. Then send it and move on — a good letter sent today beats a perfect letter next week.

Example: entry-level marketing role (Friendly tone)

\"Hi Nova team — I'm excited to apply for the Marketing Assistant role at Nova. Your 'weird works' campaign is exactly the kind of bold creative I want to make. In my internship at BrightCo I grew the TikTok account from 2k to 45k followers in six months, wrote the email series that lifted open rates 12%, and coordinated three product launches end to end. I'd love to bring that scrappy energy to Nova — can we talk? Warmly, Priya.\" 130 words, specific, human. Notice what's missing: no \"esteemed company,\" no objective statement, no adjectives without evidence.

Example: senior engineering role (Confident tone)

\"Dear Meridian Hiring Team — I'm applying for the Staff Engineer position because I build systems that scale, and I have the record to prove it: re-architected payments to handle 10x holiday traffic with zero downtime; cut infrastructure costs 38% while improving p99 latency; mentored four engineers to senior. I don't just keep systems running — I make them boringly reliable. Let's discuss what I'll do for Meridian's platform. Regards, Daniel.\" Senior letters can be shorter, not longer — authority needs fewer words.

Email application vs attachment

When the job ad says \"email your application,\" the email body is the cover letter — paste it in, attach the CV as PDF, and keep the subject line exactly as requested (\"Application: Senior Designer — Ava Sharma\"). When applying through a portal, upload the letter as a separate PDF unless the form has a dedicated text box. Never send .docx — formatting breaks across systems, and PDF looks professional everywhere.

Following up without being annoying

One follow-up, 7–10 days after applying: three sentences restating interest and offering any further information. That's it. No second follow-up, no LinkedIn stalking the hiring manager, no \"just bumping this.\" Hiring pipelines are slow for reasons unrelated to you; a single polite nudge shows enthusiasm, while repeated ones signal desperation. Meanwhile, keep applying elsewhere — the best negotiating position is another offer.

Cover letters for career changers

Career changers need the letter more than anyone — it's where the pivot gets explained. The formula: name the transferable skill explicitly (\"five years of client-facing sales taught me to diagnose needs fast — the core of UX research\"), show one concrete bridge (a project, course, or freelance work in the new field), and express genuine, specific motivation for the switch. Never apologise for the old career; reframe it as the source of your edge.

Cover letters for internal applications

Applying inside your own company? Shorter is better — they know the firm, so skip the \"why this company\" paragraph and focus on why this role and why now. Reference internal context explicitly: projects you've shipped, stakeholders who'll vouch for you, institutional knowledge you'll bring. The tone can be one notch more direct than an external application; you're a colleague making a case, not a stranger introducing yourself.

ATS and formatting basics

Most large employers run applications through Applicant Tracking Systems. Keep formatting simple: standard fonts, no tables or text boxes, no headers/footers with critical info. Use the exact role title from the job ad somewhere in the letter — it's the simplest keyword match there is. Save and upload as PDF unless the portal explicitly asks for .docx. None of this gets you hired, but it stops you getting filtered before a human ever reads a word.

Remember: hiring managers skim — put your strongest achievement in the first three lines, where eyes always land.

The pattern: a specific number, a named skill, and a direct link to the role — all inside the opening lines. Generic openers force the reader to hunt for reasons to care; strong ones hand those reasons over immediately.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a cover letter be?

250–350 words — about four short paragraphs. Hiring managers spend under a minute on first pass, so every sentence must earn its place. Over 400 words signals you can't prioritise.

What should I include in a cover letter?

Role title + one genuine company-specific reason, three proof points (achievement + scale + result), and a confident close connecting your experience to their needs. Specificity beats eloquence every time.

Which tone should my cover letter use?

Professional for finance, law, healthcare and corporates. Friendly for startups, creative and nonprofit roles. Confident for senior and sales positions. When unsure, mirror the tone of the company's job ad.

How do I write a cover letter with no direct experience?

Frame what you bring, not what you lack. Lead with transferable achievements and genuine motivation for the role — never apologise for your background or write 'I know I don't have X but…'

What are the biggest cover letter mistakes?

Repeating your CV instead of adding narrative, using the wrong company name, apologising for gaps, writing over 400 words, and generic flattery without a specific reason. Each is an instant credibility hit.

Do recruiters actually read cover letters?

Yes — as a tiebreaker. When two CVs look equal, the candidate with a specific, thoughtful letter wins. A generic template letter is worse than none, so always customise.
