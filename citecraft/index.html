    The Complete Guide to Citation Styles: APA, MLA, Chicago, and Harvard

    Every essay, research paper, thesis, and journal article stands on the shoulders of earlier work. Citations are how you acknowledge that debt. A citation tells your reader exactly which book, article, website, or video informed your argument, and it gives them enough information to find that source themselves. Done well, citations make your writing look credible, careful, and professional. Done badly — or left out entirely — they can undermine an otherwise strong paper and, in the worst case, lead to accusations of plagiarism. This guide explains the four citation styles students and researchers use most often: APA 7th edition, MLA 9th edition, Chicago 17th edition (author-date), and Harvard referencing. It also shows how a free citation generator like CiteCraft can take the tedious formatting work off your plate.

    Why citations matter

    Citations serve three purposes. First, they give credit to the original authors whose ideas, data, or words you used. Scholarship is a conversation across time, and citations are how you show you are listening to the other participants. Second, they let readers verify your claims. If you write that a particular study found a result, your reader should be able to look up that study and check. Third, they demonstrate the depth of your research. A paper with a rich, carefully formatted bibliography signals that you did the reading. Universities take this seriously: most institutions treat missing or fabricated citations as an academic integrity issue. Learning to cite correctly is therefore not just a formatting chore — it is a core research skill.

    In-text citations vs. the reference list

    All four styles covered here split the job into two parts. The in-text citation is a short marker placed inside your writing, right where you use the source. It is deliberately brief so it does not interrupt your reader's flow. The reference list (called Works Cited in MLA, and sometimes simply the bibliography) appears at the end of your document and gives the full details of every source: author, year, title, publisher, and so on. The two parts are linked — every in-text citation must point to exactly one entry in the reference list, and every entry in the list should be cited at least once in the text. A common beginner mistake is to list sources that were never cited, or to cite sources in the text that never appear in the list. A good citation generator keeps both halves consistent because it builds them from the same source details.

    There are two flavors of in-text citation. A parenthetical citation puts the whole marker in brackets at the end of the sentence: Memory improves with spaced repetition (Smith, 2020). A narrative citation weaves the author's name into your sentence and puts the rest in brackets: Smith (2020) shows that memory improves with spaced repetition. Both are correct; the choice is stylistic. CiteCraft generates both versions for every source, so you can pick whichever reads better in your paragraph.

    APA 7th edition: the social science standard

    The American Psychological Association style dominates psychology, education, business, nursing, and much of the social sciences. Its signature is the author-date system. In the text you write (Smith, 2020), and in the reference list the entry starts with the author's surname and initials, followed by the year in parentheses: Smith, J. (2020). The Great Book. Acme Press. Note the details APA cares about: only the author's initials (not full first names), the year immediately after the author, sentence case for titles (only the first word and proper nouns capitalized), and italics for book and journal titles. For journal articles, APA 7th wants the article title in plain text, the journal name and volume in italics, and a DOI formatted as a full URL: https://doi.org/10.1000/xyz123. Web pages include the site name and the URL, but no longer require a retrieval date unless the content is designed to change over time. When a work has three or more authors, APA shortens the in-text citation to the first author plus et al. — for example, (Lee et al., 2021) — while the reference list entry also uses et al. after the first author. If no author is available, the title moves into the author's position; if no date is available, APA uses the placeholder n.d. (no date).

    MLA 9th edition: the humanities choice

    The Modern Language Association style is the default in literature, languages, cultural studies, and many high-school English classes. MLA's in-text citations are famously minimal: just the author's surname, and a page number when you quote or refer to a specific passage — (Smith 23). No year, no comma. The Works Cited entry carries the weight instead: Smith, John. The Great Book. Acme Press, 2020. Notice the differences from APA: the author's full first name, the publisher before the year, and the year at the very end. MLA 9th organizes every citation around a template of nine core elements — author, title of source, title of container, contributors, version, number, publisher, publication date, and location — which makes it flexible enough to describe anything from a printed novel to a TikTok video. Containers matter: an article sits inside a journal, a video sits inside YouTube, and MLA wants both. Web sources end with the URL (without https:// in strict MLA, though including it is widely accepted) and an access date, because web pages change. Two or more authors are joined with and, and three or more become Smith, John, et al. When there is no author, the entry simply begins with the title.

    Chicago 17th author-date: history and the sciences

    The Chicago Manual of Style actually offers two systems. The notes and bibliography system uses footnotes or endnotes and is beloved in history and the arts. The author-date system, which CiteCraft generates, is preferred in the sciences and social sciences and looks close to APA at first glance: (Smith 2020) in the text. Look closer and the differences appear. There is no comma between author and year in the in-text citation, the reference list gives the author's full first name rather than initials, and the year follows the author with a period rather than sitting in parentheses: Smith, John. 2020. The Great Book. Acme Press. Journal articles put the article title in quotation marks, the journal title in italics, and the volume and pages after it, ending with the DOI or URL. Chicago is precise about dates on web content, asking for the full publication or revision date when available. Like the other styles, it shortens long author lists with et al. Chicago's flexibility is its strength — it handles archival documents, interviews, artworks, and other unusual sources gracefully — but that same flexibility is why students reach for a generator instead of memorizing the manual.

    Harvard referencing: the international author-date system

    Harvard referencing is not published by Harvard University, despite the name — it is a family of author-date styles used widely across the United Kingdom, Australia, and many international universities. Individual institutions publish their own Harvard guides, so small details vary, but the core pattern is stable: (Smith, 2020) in the text, and in the reference list Smith, J., 2020. The Great Book. Acme Press. Notice the compact author format — surname, initial, no spaces — and the year followed by a period rather than parentheses. Journal articles use single quotation marks around the article title, and web sources follow the distinctive formula Available at: URL [Accessed Day Month Year]. Harvard is strict about the access date for online sources because it tells the reader which version of a changeable page you saw. Multiple authors follow the familiar pattern: two authors joined by and, three or more shortened to et al. When no author exists, Harvard uses Anon; when no date exists, it uses no date. Because Harvard varies slightly between universities, always check your institution's guide — CiteCraft follows the widely used Cite Them Right conventions, which satisfy most requirements.

    DOIs, URLs, and the access date

    A DOI (digital object identifier) is a permanent ID assigned to most published journal articles. It never rots the way URLs do, which is why APA, MLA, Chicago, and Harvard all prefer it when one exists. You will usually find it on the article's first page or on the publisher's website, formatted like 10.1000/xyz123. In your citation, present it as a clickable link: https://doi.org/10.1000/xyz123. If there is no DOI, use the most stable URL available — the journal's page for the article rather than a database session link. For ordinary web pages and videos, the URL is the location element, and Harvard and MLA additionally want the date you accessed the page. This matters because web content changes: the access date tells your reader which version you are citing.     Common citation mistakes to avoid

    Even careful students trip on the same handful of errors. The first is mixing styles — starting a paper in APA and drifting into MLA halfway through. Pick one style and use it for every citation. The second is inconsistent author names: writing J. Smith in one entry and John Smith in another confuses both readers and alphabetical sorting. The third is forgetting italics: book titles, journal names, and website names are italicized in the reference list across all four styles, while article and chapter titles go in quotation marks (MLA, Chicago) or plain sentence case (APA). The fourth is the hanging indent — reference lists use a hanging indent where the first line starts at the margin and subsequent lines are indented. CiteCraft's output is designed with this in mind. The fifth is citing sources you never read, or listing background reading that was never cited in the text. And the sixth is trusting a generator blindly: always glance over the result, especially capitalization and author names, before submitting.

    Which style should you choose?

    If your instructor or publisher names a style, the decision is made — follow their requirement exactly, including the edition. If the choice is yours, match your discipline: APA for psychology, education, business, and the social sciences; MLA for literature, languages, and the humanities; Chicago author-date for history, the sciences, and general academic publishing; Harvard if you study at a UK, Australian, or international university that specifies it. When in doubt, ask your instructor — a thirty-second question now saves hours of reformatting later.     Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the difference between APA, MLA, Chicago, and Harvard citation styles?

    APA 7th is used in the social sciences with author-date citations like (Smith, 2020). MLA 9th is common in the humanities with author-page citations like (Smith 23). Chicago 17th author-date is popular in history and the sciences and looks like (Smith 2020). Harvard is an author-date system widely used in the UK, Australia, and international universities, formatted like (Smith, 2020) with compact reference entries.

    How do I cite a source with no author?

    Move the title into the author position. APA 7th gives: Title of Work. (2020). Publisher. MLA 9th starts with the title: Title of Work. Publisher, 2020. Harvard uses Anon: Anon, 2020. Title. Publisher. CiteCraft handles this automatically when you leave the author field blank.

    How do I cite a source with no publication date?

    Use n.d. in APA and Chicago, for example (Smith, n.d.). In MLA, omit the year and rely on the access date for web sources. In Harvard, write no date, for example Smith, J., no date. Title. Publisher. CiteCraft inserts the correct placeholder for each style when the year field is empty.

    What does et al. mean and when should I use it?

    Et al. is short for the Latin et alia, meaning "and others." Use it when a source has three or more authors: name the first author and shorten the rest. APA 7th uses Smith et al. (2020) in text, MLA 9th uses Smith, John, et al., and Harvard uses Smith, J. et al. CiteCraft applies et al. automatically for three or more authors.

    Do I need to include a DOI or URL in my citations?

    Yes, whenever one is available. APA 7th requires a DOI as https://doi.org/xxxxx for journal articles or the URL for web content. MLA 9th, Chicago, and Harvard also end entries with the DOI or URL. A DOI is preferred because it is a permanent link.
