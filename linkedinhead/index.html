Your Headline Is Your Billboard

On LinkedIn, your headline follows you everywhere: search results, comments, connection requests, recruiter inboxes. It is the single most-read line on your profile, and LinkedIn's own data shows that profiles with complete, keyword-rich headlines get dramatically more search appearances. Yet most people leave the default — their current job title at their current company — which tells a recruiter almost nothing and tells the search algorithm even less.

A strong headline does three jobs at once. It ranks — it contains the keywords recruiters actually type into LinkedIn Recruiter. It positions — it tells a stranger in five seconds what you do and who you do it for. And it differentiates — it gives one proof point that separates you from the ten thousand other people with the same title. The builder above assembles all three from your role, your sharpest skill, your years of experience, and one concrete achievement.

The Anatomy of a High-Performing Headline

Great headlines share a structure, even when the words differ. The most reliable formula is:

Role | Skill positioning | Proof of value

For example: "Product Designer | Design Systems Specialist | 6 yrs — Shipped checkout used by 2M shoppers." Each segment earns its place. The role matches the job titles recruiters search. The skill positioning narrows you to a specialty, which paradoxically gets you more relevant inbound, not less. The proof — a number, a launch, a recognizable name — is what makes a hiring manager click instead of scrolling past.

You have 220 characters to work with, which is generous — most effective headlines use 120–180. Shorter than 80 characters and you are probably wasting ranking potential; longer than 200 and you risk looking cluttered on mobile, where headlines truncate aggressively.

Keywords: Write for the Search Box, Not the Mirror

Recruiters do not search LinkedIn the way you describe yourself at dinner parties. They type job titles and skill keywords: "product designer," "design systems," "SaaS," "B2B." Your headline should contain the exact phrases from the job descriptions you want. This is not keyword stuffing — it is translation. If your official title is "Product Design Wizard" but the market hires "Product Designers," your headline should say Product Designer.

Pull up five job postings you would love and circle the repeated nouns. Those nouns belong in your headline. The keyword chips above offer common high-value terms to spark ideas, but your target job descriptions are the ultimate source of truth. Aim for your primary role keyword and one or two specialty keywords to appear naturally in the line — that density check is part of the scorer above.

Separators and Scannability

The vertical bar — | — is the headline's workhorse. It visually chunks the line so a skimming recruiter can parse it in under two seconds, and it signals intentional structure rather than a rambling sentence. Alternatives like middots (·) or em dashes work, but the pipe is the convention recruiters' eyes are trained on.

Two formatting rules matter. First, front-load the most important keyword: LinkedIn's search algorithm and human skimmers both weight the beginning of the line more heavily. Your role title should come first, not your philosophy of work. Second, never bury the proof at the end if it is your strongest asset — a headline that opens with a metric ("Grew ARR $2M → $11M | VP Sales") can outperform a title-first version for senior roles. The A/B variant generator above produces alternative orderings so you can compare.

What to Leave Out

  • "Open to work" as the whole headline. The #OpenToWork photo frame already signals availability; spending headline characters on it wastes ranking space. Put your value proposition there instead.
  • Buzzword soup. "Passionate results-driven ninja guru" says nothing searchable and reads as filler. One vivid proof point beats five adjectives.
  • Your entire job history. The headline is not a resume. Current positioning plus proof is enough; the experience section carries the rest.
  • Contact info. Email addresses and phone numbers in headlines look spammy and LinkedIn may suppress them. Keep contact details in the contact section.
  • All caps. It reads as shouting and truncates worse on mobile. Title case or sentence case throughout.

How the 100-Point Scorer Works

The scorer above grades your headline on the three factors that actually drive performance. Length (40 points): full marks for staying within LinkedIn's 220-character limit with room to breathe — headlines in the 100–200 range score best, since they use the space without truncating on mobile. Keyword density (30 points): checks that your role, skill, and proof terms actually appear in the final line — a headline that drops the keywords you typed is leaking search traffic. Separator structure (30 points): rewards the pipe-delimited chunking that makes headlines scannable in search results and feeds.

Treat 80+ as publish-ready. Between 60 and 80, fix whichever dimension is weakest — usually it is keyword density, which is a five-second fix. Below 60, rebuild from the formula rather than patching: role first, specialty second, proof third.

Testing With A/B Variants

You cannot truly A/B test a LinkedIn headline the way you test an ad — LinkedIn shows one headline at a time. But you can rotate deliberately. The variant generator produces three alternative wordings: a specialty-first version, a proof-first version, and a conversational version. Run each for two to three weeks and watch two metrics: search appearances (Profile → Analytics) and recruiter inbound (how many relevant messages you get).

Change one variable at a time. If the proof-first variant doubles your inbound, you have learned something durable about your market: outcomes sell harder than titles in your field. Keep a note of what you tested and when — the highest-performing headline is an asset you will reuse across your resume summary, portfolio hero, and personal site.

Keeping It Fresh

A headline is not a tattoo. Update it whenever your positioning changes: a promotion, a completed certification, a shipped launch with a number worth bragging about, or a pivot toward a new target role. Job seekers should refresh the headline at the start of a search with the keywords of the roles they are targeting — this alone can multiply search appearances within days. And once a quarter, re-read your headline as a stranger would: if it takes more than five seconds to understand what you do and why you are good at it, tighten it.

Build the line, score it, generate the variants, and ship the winner. Your headline works while you sleep — give it something worth saying.

A 10-Minute Headline Rewrite

If your current headline is just a job title, here is a ten-minute upgrade path. Minutes 1-3: open three job postings you want and list the repeated role and skill nouns - those are your keywords. Minutes 4-6: type your role, your sharpest skill, and your years into the builder, then add the single most impressive number from your last two years of work. Minutes 7-8: check the score; if keyword density is low, swap vague words for the exact posting nouns. Minutes 9-10: generate the A/B variants, pick the strongest, copy it to LinkedIn, and set a reminder to review search appearances in three weeks.

That is the whole system: keywords from real postings, a scannable three-part structure, one proof point, and deliberate iteration. Most professionals see a visible lift in profile views within the first month - not because of tricks, but because the headline finally does the three jobs it was always supposed to do.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a LinkedIn headline be?

220 characters. The most effective headlines use roughly 120–180 — enough to include role, specialty, and proof keywords without truncating on mobile. The scorer above gives full length marks in that range.

Which keywords should I put in my headline?

Use the exact titles and skill phrases from job postings you want. Recruiters search for 'product designer' and 'SQL', not clever internal titles. Your headline should translate how you describe yourself into what the market searches for.

Should I use the | separator in my headline?

Yes. The pipe (|) visually chunks the headline so recruiters can parse it in about two seconds, and it's the convention their eyes are trained on. Keep your most important keyword before the first separator.

Should my headline say I'm open to work?

Only the photo-frame badge, not headline space. Writing 'Open to Work' in the headline wastes characters that could carry searchable keywords. Put your value proposition in the headline and let the badge signal availability.

How often should I update my headline?

Refresh it at the start of every job search with your target roles' keywords, and whenever your positioning changes — a promotion, certification, or launch with a number worth sharing. Quarterly, re-read it as a stranger: if it takes more than five seconds to grasp, tighten it.

Do emojis belong in a LinkedIn headline?

Sparingly, if at all. One restrained emoji can add personality in creative fields, but they consume character budget, render inconsistently across devices, and can read as unprofessional in conservative industries. Keywords and proof outperform decoration.
